Why most AI pilots never reach production

The model is rarely the problem. The pilot was usually picked for the wrong reasons, and nobody agreed what success looks like.

Osama Elmelegy11 November 2026 · 5 min read

Over the last two years I've sat in a lot of rooms where an organization proudly shows its AI pilot. A chatbot, a document classifier, a dashboard with a forecast line. Six months later, most of them are still pilots.

When I look back at why, the reasons are almost never technical. The model worked well enough. What didn't work was everything around it.

1. The use case was picked because it was easy to demo

A chatbot over a policy document is quick to build and looks impressive in a meeting. But if the people answering those questions today spend two hours a week on it, the business case is tiny. Pilots should start where the pain is measurable: hours spent, cases backlogged, errors that cost money.

2. Nobody owned the outcome

IT owned the pilot and the business "supported" it. When it was time to change a real process, nobody on the business side had the mandate to do it. Every use case I take forward now has a named business owner before we write a line of code.

3. Success was never defined

"Let's see what AI can do" is not a success criterion. Before a proof of concept starts, I agree on two or three numbers with the client: time per case, accuracy against a human reviewer, share of requests handled without escalation. If we hit them, we scale. If not, we stop without arguing.

4. The data question came too late

Many pilots run on a clean sample that someone prepared by hand. Production runs on the messy reality. I now ask in the first workshop where the data lives, who owns it, and whether it can legally be used for this purpose. In government work, that last question often decides everything.

5. There was no path to production

A pilot built in a notebook on someone's laptop has nowhere to go. The architecture, hosting and integration choices need to be made with production in mind from day one, even if the first version is small.

What I do differently now

I treat the use case selection as the most important deliverable. A ranked shortlist, each item with an owner, a measurable goal, a data check and a rough cost, is worth more than any demo. The proof of concept then becomes a test of a clear hypothesis, not a showcase.

AI adoption in the Kingdom is moving fast, and the organizations that win won't be the ones with the most pilots. They'll be the ones that picked three good ones and took them all the way.
